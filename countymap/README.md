# Regions by county

A third map of the region scheme, aimed at someone meeting it for the first time.
`/map` answers "what should *this point* carry"; this one answers "which counties
are in my region", because that is the unit people already argue in — especially
across eastern Washington, where the mesh is thin and a region is discussed as a
bundle of counties rather than a radius.

```bash
php -S localhost:8080       # then open /countymap/
```

## The honest bit

**The scheme contains no counties.** A location resolves by weighted Voronoi —
`argmin(distKm - r)` — which is continuous and pays no attention to survey lines.
So this view does not assert a county-to-region mapping; it **measures** one.
Every county is sampled on a grid, every sample point is resolved through the
shared engine, and the region that wins the most of the county's area is the one
it is drawn as.

That measurement is also the safeguard. Where a county genuinely straddles two
regions the tally says so, and the county is drawn in both regions' colours as
diagonal stripes rather than being quietly rounded off. It is not a rare case:

| Runner-up holds | Counties |
|---|---|
| ≥ 40% of the county | 9 |
| ≥ 30% (drawn as shared) | 23 |
| ≥ 20% | 36 |

Jefferson and Clallam are 52/48 between `sea` and `grh`; Pend Oreille is 52/48
between `geg` and `cda`. Presenting those as belonging to one region would be a
real misstatement, so the map doesn't.

The **RF coverage** toggle is the other half of the honesty. It paints the actual
weighted-Voronoi result in the same colours, clipped to the same counties, with
the county lines still drawn on top. Flipping between the two modes is the point:
the bundles are close, and where they aren't, you can see exactly where.

## Reading the map

- **Hue is the state, shade separates neighbours.** Washington reads blue, Oregon
  orange, Idaho teal, Montana pink. Colouring regions arbitrarily would waste the
  strongest channel on the map; this way every fill also answers "which state is
  this", and a cross-border community spanning two hues explains itself. Within a
  state, adjacent bundles only need to differ — the classic map-colouring problem,
  which needs four shades at most.
- **Heavy outlines are region bundles**, drawn by hashing every boundary segment
  and keeping the ones whose two counties resolve to different regions. Exact, not
  approximate — see the topology note below.
- **Dashed outlines are cross-border communities** (`inw`, `palouse`, …), each drawn
  as one shape across the state line. This is the clearest rendering of that idea
  anywhere in the repo: a real place on a real map, not a rail or a matrix column.
  They are drawn **all at once**, because they nest — `palouse`'s areas are a strict
  subset of `inw`'s, and showing one at a time erased the inner one entirely even
  though `palouse`'s parent *is* `inw`. An overlay with only one member region is
  left off: its outline would just be that region's own bundle drawn twice.
- **Dots are region centres** — the seeds the resolver actually measures from.

## Where the data comes from

Region data is read at runtime from [`regions.json`](../regions.json) through
[`shared/region-engine.js`](../shared/region-engine.js), and cross-border
membership through the visualizer's
[`model.js`](../visualizer/src/model.js) — so nothing here duplicates the scheme,
and a new tag or rule shows up without a code change.

Boundaries are the one piece of outside data:
[`public/boundaries.json`](public/boundaries.json), ~290 KB, generated by
[`scripts/build-boundaries.mjs`](scripts/build-boundaries.mjs). It is committed, so
serving the page needs no build step and no network. Regenerate it only to refresh
the boundaries or widen the area — it holds no region data and never goes stale
when `regions.json` changes.

| Layer | Source | Licence |
|---|---|---|
| US counties + state outlines | [us-atlas](https://github.com/topojson/us-atlas) (Natural Earth 1:10m) | public domain |
| BC regional districts | [DataBC](https://catalogue.data.gov.bc.ca/dataset/d1aff64e-dbfe-45a6-af97-582b7f6418b9) WFS | Open Government Licence – BC |
| Land outline (sea mask) | [world-atlas](https://github.com/topojson/world-atlas) land-10m | public domain |

**Topology is the hard part.** Bundle outlines are drawn by hashing every boundary
segment and keeping the ones whose two areas resolve to different regions, which
only works while neighbours share byte-identical vertices.

- US counties arrive as TopoJSON, so the build simplifies at the **arc** level and
  the topology is preserved by construction.
- BC arrives as loose WFS polygons, so the build reconstructs it: rings are split
  into chains at the junctions where the set of districts using the boundary
  changes, and each *chain* is thinned rather than each ring. The run between two
  junctions is the same points on both sides, and Douglas–Peucker is
  direction-symmetric, so both sides thin identically with no coordination.

Two things that look like they would work and do not. Simplifying per polygon
thins each side of a shared border differently and leaves hairline gaps across the
map. Merely *protecting* shared vertices keeps the topology exact but leaves BC at
twenty times the size it needs, because the districts tile the province and nearly
all their vertices sit on interior borders that then never thin — 356k vertices
down to 259k. Chain-level thinning gets the same file to 5k, a 98% cut, with the
topology still exact.

Also worth knowing: Douglas–Peucker on a closed ring degenerates, because the
first and last points coincide and the base chord has no length. Rings are cut at
their two most distant points and thinned in halves.

## British Columbia, and why the coastline matters

BC has regional districts rather than counties; they come from
[DataBC](https://catalogue.data.gov.bc.ca/dataset/d1aff64e-dbfe-45a6-af97-582b7f6418b9)
under the Open Government Licence – British Columbia.

They needed one extra step. `vanisle` and `swbc` are **geographic** names —
Vancouver Island, the Lower Mainland — but a regional district is a *legal* area
and owns a great deal of open water. Drawn as delivered, `vanisle` was a shape
covering half the Strait of Georgia and looked nothing like the island it is named
after. US coastal counties have the same problem more mildly.

So the build also keeps a land outline (Natural Earth 10m, via
[world-atlas](https://github.com/topojson/world-atlas)) clipped to the map window,
and the page lays a **sea mask** over the fills: one rectangle with every landmass
punched out of it. Region fills then stop at the coast, and the geographically
named tags look like the geography.

The Gulf Islands are the remaining limit. `salishmesh` and `southisland` are
sub-areas of districts that also contain a lot of Vancouver Island, so at district
granularity they rarely win a bundle of their own — the **RF coverage** mode is the
honest view for them.

## Distance, and what this map does not decide

Fill strength falls off with distance from the region's centre, counted in that
region's own `r`. It is a ramp rather than a cutoff on purpose: the resolver
answers everywhere, so "covered" is a judgement for the mesh and not for a map.
Every threshold tested either washed out half of eastern Oregon or let eastern BC
keep a confident `swbc` fill from 250 km away. A ramp states the distance without
ruling on it — and it makes the thin parts of the mesh visible, which is most of
eastern Oregon, southern Idaho, south-west Montana and everything in BC east of
Hope.
